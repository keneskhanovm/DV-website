Links to the datasets and reports used in our analysis:

Dataset What it contributes
OWID: How much energy do data centers and AI use? Main reference. Data centres used about 485 TWh in 2025 (around 1.5% of global electricity), of which AI-focused ones used about 155 TWh, and the IEA base case reaches 945 TWh (3%) in 2030.
OWID chart: share of electricity demand from data centers Downloadable data for the first burden indicator. It combines IEA data centre consumption with Ember total electricity demand.
IEA, Energy and AI Regional data centre electricity use and scenarios to 2030. In 2024 the US accounted for 45% of global data centre electricity, China 25% and Europe 15%.
Ember, Yearly Electricity Data Yearly electricity generation, capacity, emissions, imports and demand for over 200 geographies. Gives the denominator and the grid carbon intensity for the CO2 estimate.
WRI Aqueduct 4.0 The baseline water stress indicator, comparing total water demand with available renewable supply. Gives water stress by country and region.
DataCenterMap Number and location of data centres, to match with water stress. Used in a Planet Tracker analysis of data centres and water stress.
UN News: AI’s environmental costs Summary of the UN University study. AI-related water consumption could equal the basic annual domestic needs of 1.3 billion people by the end of the decade. Useful for context and the “who bears the burden” argument.
JRC: EU Code of Conduct for data centres and Eurostat Optional European zoom. New EU reporting rules cover energy consumption, water consumption and renewable energy use for data centres. Eurostat gives total electricity demand and the mix.
OWID: Scaling up AI Explains why demand grows. Training compute has doubled roughly every six months since 2010. One context chart for change over time.

Concerns about data

  • AI is mixed with other data centre use: The data does not allow a separate estimate for AI use, so most charts will show “data centres”. The IEA’s AI-focused split (about a third of the total in 2025) can be shown once, at global level.
  • Sources disagree: The Energy Institute/S&P estimates about 790 TWh for 2025 (including crypto mining), against the IEA’s 485 TWh. We will use the IEA figures and state why.
  • Country coverage: The IEA-based data covers the world, the US, China and large regions (such as Europe, Africa and the Middle East), not every country. A country-level map may need data centre counts instead of electricity.
  • Water data is weak: Most data centres do not report their water consumption, and less than a third of operators tracked any water metrics. We will show water stress at data centre locations, not measured water use.
  • CO2 is estimated, not reported: We will multiply electricity by the average carbon intensity of the local grid. Company-reported emissions can differ, since offsets and certificates change the accounting. We will state the method on the page.
  • Location matters: Country-level data hides large variation within countries, and water supply is mostly local.
  • Projections are uncertain: We will show scenario ranges, not one line.
  • Licensing: IEA data has usage terms and DataCenterMap is commercial, so we need to check what we can download and republish. Our World in Data and Ember are the safest base.
